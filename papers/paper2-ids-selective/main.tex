\begin{abstract}
A machine-learning intrusion detector that emits one hard label gives no signal of when to trust it and
fails silently on attacks absent from training. We ask whether off-the-shelf detectors know when they
are wrong---we propose no new detector. Under a controlled \emph{leave-one-family-out} (LOFO) protocol on
the public NSL-KDD benchmark, detection of a held-out attack family collapses (e.g.\ DoS from
\PH{det_seen_DoS} to \PH{det_unseen_DoS}); a confidence-based reject option recovers only part of the
gap, and almost none when the attack mimics benign traffic (R2L), which a benign-only novelty detector
also cannot cleanly separate---a \emph{structural} blind spot, not a tuning issue. The calibration
measurements support this picture: the three standard detectors are markedly over-confident under the
benchmark's train--test shift (expected calibration error up to 0.22), post-hoc Platt scaling
fitted on source data does not transfer, and split-conformal coverage falls from 0.93 to
0.61. Selective prediction nonetheless cuts selective risk from 0.20 to
0.11 at \mbox{80\%} coverage---but only for models whose confidence ranks errors (tree
ensembles, not logistic regression: area under the risk--coverage curve 0.05 versus
0.23). On a second, modern corpus (CIC-IDS-2017/2018) the within-distribution task is easy and
well calibrated, yet an unknown family is still missed under cross-day and cross-corpus drift---so
over-confidence is task-dependent while the abstention blind spot is not. Selective prediction is a
cheap, model-agnostic safety layer for ML intrusion detection, with clearly characterised limits.
\end{abstract}

A network intrusion detector that emits only ``attack'' or ``benign'' gives an operator no way to tell
a confident verdict from a guess. In practice this matters twice over: alerts compete for scarce
analyst attention, and genuinely novel attacks---absent from any training corpus---are exactly the
cases a static classifier is least equipped to label. A detector that could say ``I am not sure'' on
these inputs, and defer them to a human or a secondary control (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}), would be more
useful than one forced to commit.

This paper asks a narrow, practical question: do off-the-shelf machine-learning intrusion detectors know
when they are wrong---producing confidence scores trustworthy enough to drive an abstention policy, and
behaving safely on attacks they have never seen? \emph{We propose no new detector; we evaluate existing
ones.} We answer empirically on NSL-KDD \cite{tavallaee2009nsl}, whose published test split deliberately
contains attack types absent from training, and confirm the phenomena on a modern flow corpus
(CIC-IDS-2017/2018) under cross-day and cross-corpus drift (Section~\ref{sec:cic}). Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item We introduce a controlled \emph{leave-one-family-out} (LOFO) open-set protocol---released as
anonymized, reusable code (open-licensed at camera-ready)---and show that detection of a held-out attack
family collapses, that a confidence reject option covers only part of the gap, and that attacks mimicking
benign traffic (R2L) form a \emph{structural} blind spot, missed by abstention and by a benign-only
novelty stage alike (Section~\ref{sec:openset}).
\item We show selective prediction \cite{elyaniv2010selective,geifman2017selective} cuts selective risk
substantially, but only for models whose confidence ranks their errors---tree ensembles, not the linear
model---and that split-conformal coverage erodes under the same shift (Section~\ref{sec:sp}).
\item As the mechanism behind both, we measure that the three standard detectors are over-confident under
distribution shift and that post-hoc Platt scaling \cite{platt1999probabilistic} fitted on source data
does \emph{not} transfer (Section~\ref{sec:cal}).
\end{itemize}
Throughout we follow the evaluation guidance of Arp \emph{et al.}\ \cite{arp2022dodos}: the calibrator
is fit only on held-out training data, and no test information leaks into model selection. Unless stated
otherwise, NSL-KDD test metrics are reported as the mean over $K{=}15$ seeds---resampling both the
fit/calibration split and model randomness---with 95\% confidence intervals (the canonical test
partition is held fixed, so these intervals reflect train/model variance); equivalence is assessed with
the \emph{paired} per-seed difference and its CI rather than overlapping marginal intervals.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Calibration.} Modern classifiers often output probabilities that do not match empirical
accuracy \cite{guo2017calibration,niculescu2005predicting}. Expected calibration error (ECE) summarises
this gap, and temperature/Platt scaling are common post-hoc fixes \cite{platt1999probabilistic}. These
analyses typically assume the evaluation data is drawn from the training distribution---an assumption
that fails for intrusion detection.

\textbf{Selective prediction.} A classifier with a reject option \cite{elyaniv2010selective} trades
coverage for accuracy on what it does accept; the risk--coverage curve and its area
\cite{geifman2017selective} quantify the trade-off. Maximum softmax/posterior probability is a strong
baseline confidence score \cite{hendrycks2017baseline}.

\textbf{ML for intrusion detection.} The limits of learned detectors---especially on novel attacks and
under distribution shift---were argued early \cite{sommer2010outside} and remain a methodological
hazard \cite{arp2022dodos}. We do not propose a new detector; we ask whether existing ones know when
they are wrong.

\textbf{Unknown attacks and shift.} The failure of closed-world classifiers on inputs unlike their
training data motivates out-of-distribution and open-set detection, for which the maximum softmax
probability is the standard baseline \cite{hendrycks2017baseline}. NSL-KDD operationalises this within
a security benchmark: its test split deliberately contains attack types absent from
training~\cite{tavallaee2009nsl}, so measuring behaviour on them needs no synthetic construction. Our
family-holdout protocol complements that built-in novelty with a controlled version that makes the
``unknown'' condition causal. We deliberately reuse only off-the-shelf scores and standard metrics so
that the conclusions concern the \emph{evaluation} of detectors, not a new detector design.

\section{Methodology}
\textbf{Data.} NSL-KDD \cite{tavallaee2009nsl} provides \PH{n_train} training and \PH{n_test} test
records with 41 features (three categorical) and a binary normal/attack label; each attack maps to one
of four families (DoS, Probe, R2L, U2R). Its test split contains \PH{n_novel_types} attack \emph{types}
(\PH{n_novel_rows} records) that never appear in training---a built-in open-set test. Numeric features
are standardised and categorical features one-hot encoded; the encoder is fit on training data only.

\textbf{Models.} We use scikit-learn \cite{pedregosa2011scikit} logistic regression, a random forest,
and histogram gradient boosting---deliberately standard detectors, since our subject is their
confidence, not their architecture.

\textbf{Calibration.} For each model we report ECE (15 equal-width bins of the predicted class
probability) before and after Platt scaling, the latter fitted on a 20\% held-out split of the
training set.

\textbf{Selective prediction.} The confidence of a prediction is its maximum class probability. Sorting
the test set by confidence and accepting the most-confident fraction $c$ yields the selective risk
$r(c)$; we report the area under $r(c)$ (AURC) and $r(0.8)$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:rc}).

\caption{Risk--coverage on NSL-KDD; the ring marks the 80\%-coverage operating point and the shaded

\textbf{Open-set protocol (LOFO).} To probe genuinely unknown attacks under control, our
leave-one-family-out protocol removes one attack family entirely from training (keeping benign traffic
and the other families), retrains, and measures detection and confidence on that family's test records.
We also report, at a global threshold giving 80\% coverage, the fraction of unknown-family records that
abstention rejects. Reported metrics are means over multiple seeds with 95\% confidence intervals.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Detectors are over-confident}\label{sec:cal}
All three detectors are markedly over-confident on the benchmark's shifted test set, with ECE from
$\approx$0.16 (random forest) to $\approx$0.22 (logistic regression) (Table~\ref{tab:main}).
Crucially this is induced by distribution shift, not intrinsic: on a held-out
\emph{in-distribution} split the same models are nearly perfectly calibrated (ECE $\lesssim 0.003$),
and Platt scaling keeps them there. It is only on the shifted test set---which introduces novel attacks---that ECE
inflates (Fig.~\ref{fig:eceshift}), and there post-hoc Platt scaling fit on source data cannot remove
it---ECE stays essentially unchanged for gradient boosting and is slightly worse for the random forest.
Calibrating to the source distribution does not certify
probabilities once the deployed distribution moves (Fig.~\ref{fig:rel}); this is the core reason we
turn to abstention, which needs only a usable confidence \emph{ranking}, not calibrated probabilities.

\subsection{Abstention helps---if confidence is informative}\label{sec:sp}
Abstaining on the least-confident inputs lowers selective risk---but only when the model's confidence
ranks its errors (Fig.~\ref{fig:rc}). For gradient boosting, rejecting the least-confident
20\% of inputs lowers selective risk to \PH{ms_histgb_risk80} (AURC \PH{ms_histgb_aurc}); the random
forest is similar (AURC \PH{ms_rf_aurc}). Logistic regression is the cautionary case: its confidence
barely ranks its errors (AURC \PH{ms_logreg_aurc}), so abstention buys little (\PH{ms_logreg_risk80}).
The lesson is that selective prediction is only as good as the underlying confidence signal, which here
depends strongly on the model family.

\subsection{Leave-one-family-out (LOFO) open-set evaluation}\label{sec:openset}
Detection of an attack family collapses once that family is unknown at training time, and a confidence
reject option only partly compensates. We establish this causally with a \emph{leave-one-family-out}
(LOFO) protocol: for each attack family $F$, remove all of $F$ from training (keeping benign traffic and
the other families), retrain, and measure detection and abstention on $F$'s test records---released as
anonymized, reusable code for review (open-licensed at camera-ready). On NSL-KDD's built-in novel attacks the
random forest flags ${\approx}0.77$ of \emph{known} attacks but only ${\approx}0.23$ of novel ones;
LOFO (Table~\ref{tab:openset}, Fig.~\ref{fig:open}) confirms this causally: detection of DoS falls from
\PH{det_seen_DoS} to \PH{det_unseen_DoS} when DoS is unknown, and Probe from \PH{det_seen_Probe} to
\PH{det_unseen_Probe}, with abstention rejecting \PH{reject_unseen_DoS} and \PH{reject_unseen_Probe} at
80\% coverage. The rare U2R family (train $n{=}52$, test $n{=}67$---a high-variance estimate) behaves
like the anomalous families: abstention still flags \PH{reject_unseen_U2R} of unknown U2R. R2L is the
sharp exception---detected at only \PH{det_seen_R2L} even when \emph{seen}, \PH{det_unseen_R2L} when
unknown, with abstention flagging just \PH{reject_unseen_R2L}. Because R2L traffic statistically
resembles benign sessions the detector is confidently wrong, so the blind spot is specifically
\emph{benign-mimicking} attacks, not unknown attacks in general---R2L sits inside the benign cloud for
two independent benign-only detectors alike (Fig.~\ref{fig:manifold}). The CIC-IDS-2017 cross-day
experiment below is the same protocol on a second corpus, with the Web family held out.

\subsection{Calibration is task-dependent; the blind spot is not (CIC-IDS-2017)}\label{sec:cic}
On an easier modern corpus the over-confidence disappears, yet an unknown family is still missed under
drift---calibration is a property of the task, the abstention blind spot is not. We repeat the study on
CIC-IDS-2017, a modern flow-based corpus (78 features),
using three days (DDoS, PortScan, and Web attacks). The picture is instructively different
(Table~\ref{tab:cic}). Within a random split the task is far easier than NSL-KDD---gradient boosting
reaches accuracy \PH{cic_histgb_acc} at ECE \PH{cic_histgb_ece}---so over-confidence is \emph{not} a
problem here: calibration quality is a property of how hard and how shifted the task is, not an
intrinsic flaw of the models. The value of abstention, however, persists under drift. We evaluate it
with a cross-day split---training and testing on different days rather than a random mix---which avoids
the optimistic temporal bias a random split would introduce~\cite{pendlebury2019tesseract}. Training on
Friday (DDoS, PortScan) and testing on Thursday---whose Web-attack family is unknown and whose benign
traffic has drifted---detection of Web falls from \PH{cic_web_seen} (seen) to \PH{cic_web_unseen}
(unknown); a confidence threshold at 80\% coverage then rejects \PH{cic_drift_reject_web} of those
unknown-Web records, far more than the analogous NSL-KDD case because Web flows, unlike R2L, do not
mimic benign traffic. The contrast across the two corpora is the substantive point: the calibration
failure is task-dependent, while selective prediction is broadly useful---most when the unknown attack
is statistically distinguishable from benign traffic.

\textbf{Cross-corpus transfer.} The strongest external-validity test trains on CIC-2017 and tests on
CSE-CIC-IDS-2018 (benign + Infiltration), a different network and year, using the \PH{xfer_n_common}
CICFlowMeter features whose names match exactly across the two releases. The mechanisms reappear but
degrade honestly under the real domain shift: accuracy falls from \PH{xfer_in2017_acc} (within-2017,
same features) to \PH{xfer_acc}, calibration collapses (ECE \PH{xfer_ece}), and the confidence signal
weakens (AURC \PH{xfer_aurc} vs.\ \PH{xfer_in2017_aurc}), so abstention recovers far less than
within-corpus (selective risk \PH{xfer_risk_full}$\to$\PH{xfer_risk80} at 80\% coverage). Infiltration,
an unknown and benign-mimicking attack, is the cross-corpus blind spot: detected at only
\PH{xfer_infil_detect} and rejected by abstention at \PH{xfer_infil_reject}. Cross-corpus generalization
is thus partial---the qualitative phenomena transfer, the quantitative guarantees do not (means over
$K{=}5$ seeds; 95\% CI half-width $\le\PH{xfer_maxhw}$).

\subsection{Which uncertainty signal, and can we guarantee coverage?}
Two questions sharpen the story: is maximum class probability (MSP) the right abstention signal, and can
abstention carry a formal guarantee? For the first, we benchmark MSP against random-forest ensemble
disagreement, Mahalanobis distance to class-conditional Gaussians, and $k$NN distance, on error ranking
(AURC) and unknown-attack separation (Table~\ref{tab:signals}). The appropriate statistic for a
no-difference claim is the \emph{paired} per-seed $\Delta\text{AURC}=\text{signal}-\text{MSP}$, and it
shows that no signal dominates. Ensemble disagreement is statistically indistinguishable from MSP
(paired $\Delta$AURC \PH{ms_dmsp_rf_disagreement}; CI includes 0). Mahalanobis is marginally but
significantly \emph{better} at error ranking (\PH{ms_dmsp_mahalanobis}) yet significantly \emph{worse}
at unknown-attack detection (AUROC \PH{ms_sig_mahalanobis_unk} vs.\ MSP's \PH{ms_sig_msp_unk}); $k$NN is
worse on both (\PH{ms_dmsp_knn}). So contrary to the deep-learning out-of-distribution literature,
where richer signals dominate under shift~\cite{ovadia2019,lee2018mahalanobis}, on tabular flow data the
cheap max-softmax is a strong, well-rounded default---the leverage is in the abstention framework, not
the estimator.

For the second, we wrap the detector in split conformal prediction~\cite{angelopoulos2023conformal},
which gives distribution-free coverage under exchangeability. At target coverage $0.90$, the prediction
sets attain empirical coverage \PH{ms_conf_indist} in-distribution---valid---but only
\PH{ms_conf_shift} under the train--test shift. This is the conformal analogue of the calibration
failure of Section~\ref{sec:cal}: the guarantee is exact under exchangeability and erodes precisely when
the deployment distribution moves~\cite{tibshirani2019}. Restoring it needs shift-aware (weighted)
conformal with estimable likelihood ratios---impractical for genuine zero-days, the honest caveat.

\subsection{A benign-only novelty stage partially closes the blind spot}
Confidence-based abstention barely flags R2L (Section~\ref{sec:openset}) because such attacks lie on the
benign feature manifold (Fig.~\ref{fig:manifold}). The apt detector for that regime is a \emph{benign-only} density model---a
one-class novelty detector, distinct from the two-class Mahalanobis used above for error ranking. We fit
an Isolation Forest~\cite{liu2008isolation} and a one-class SVM~\cite{scholkopf2001oneclass} on benign
records only. At a 10\% benign false-positive budget the Isolation Forest recovers
\PH{nov_if_rec_U2R_fp10} of U2R and \PH{nov_if_rec_R2L_fp10} of R2L (the one-class SVM
\PH{nov_ocsvm_rec_U2R_fp10} and \PH{nov_ocsvm_rec_R2L_fp10}; means over $K{=}8$ seeds, 95\% CI
half-width $\le\PH{nov_maxhw}$)---against abstention's $\approx\PH{reject_unseen_R2L}$ for unknown R2L,
a clear if partial gain. The AUROC ceilings
(Fig.~\ref{fig:novelty}: R2L \PH{nov_if_auroc_R2L}, U2R \PH{nov_if_auroc_U2R}) show the limit is real but
not absolute: R2L is hardest because its support most overlaps benign, so no benign-only detector fully
recovers it, yet a novelty stage is a cheap, measured complement---abstention and novelty catch
different failure modes (confident errors vs.\ benign-mimicking attacks).

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
Selective prediction is a cheap, model-agnostic safety layer: at the cost of deferring a minority of
inputs it materially lowers the error among those acted upon, and it partially flags unknown attacks.
But our results bound the claim. First, the value of abstention is inherited from the confidence
signal, which is good for tree ensembles and poor for the linear model. Second, post-hoc calibration
on source data does not survive distribution shift, so calibrated probabilities should not be trusted
as deployment-time guarantees. Third, and most important operationally, abstention only catches
unknowns that \emph{look} anomalous; attacks that mimic benign traffic (R2L here) are missed
confidently and silently.

\emph{Operational recipe.} Deploy a model whose confidence is informative (low validation AURC---a tree
ensemble here, not the linear model); set the operating coverage from the risk--coverage curve to meet a
target precision; route the abstained minority to a secondary control; monitor the abstention rate as a
cheap, label-free drift signal~\cite{jordaney2017transcend,yang2021cade}; and pair abstention with the
benign-only novelty stage above for the benign-mimicking attacks it cannot catch.

\emph{Limitations.} NSL-KDD is dated and its features hand-crafted, so absolute numbers will differ on
modern corpora; the mechanisms we isolate---over-confidence under shift, model-dependent confidence
quality, and the benign-mimicry blind spot---are not specific to it. We study binary detection with a
single abstention signal (maximum class probability); richer signals and the multi-class
attack-typing setting are natural extensions. We evaluate a cross-day split and a cross-corpus transfer
(CIC-2017$\to$2018) on shared features; a full time-ordered evaluation, the label-corrected
CIC-2017~\cite{engelen2021troubleshooting}, and standardized NetFlow-v2 multi-network transfer (a common
schema avoiding our 27-feature restriction) remain future work.

\section{Conclusion}
On a hard benchmark (NSL-KDD) off-the-shelf intrusion detectors are over-confident and post-hoc
calibration does not transfer under shift; on an easier modern corpus (CIC-IDS-2017) they are
well-calibrated, so the need for calibration is task-dependent. Across both, a simple reject option
lowers selective risk and flags unknown attacks---strongly when those attacks are distinguishable from
benign traffic, weakly when they mimic it. Selective prediction is worth adding to ML intrusion
detection, provided its confidence signal and its blind spots are measured rather than assumed.
