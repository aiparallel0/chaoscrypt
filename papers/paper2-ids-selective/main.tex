\begin{abstract}
Machine-learning network intrusion detectors are routinely deployed against attack families they never saw
in training---open-set distribution shift. When that shift is mishandled the cost propagates: a missed
novel attack reaches production before any analyst reviews it, and the much-reported ``detection collapse''
on unknown families has been read as a representational dead end. In standard practice a detector emits
only ``attack'' or ``benign'' and leans on a single confidence score to flag uncertain cases, but that
signal cannot tell a confident verdict from a guess, and nothing makes the detector behave safely on inputs
unlike its training data. We propose no new detector---the diagnosis itself is the contribution---and
instead ask whether off-the-shelf detectors know when they are wrong, on dated NSL-KDD (whose test split
deliberately contains attack types absent from training) and on two modern flow corpora (CIC-IDS-2017/2018)
under cross-day and cross-corpus drift, and report three findings. (i) \emph{Over-confidence is
task-dependent}: on NSL-KDD the three standard detectors are badly miscalibrated under train--test shift
and no post-hoc calibrator (Platt, isotonic, temperature) transfers, whereas on the two modern corpora the
same task is well calibrated (ECE and Brier agree). (ii) \emph{The blind spot is an operating-point
failure}, not a representational one---minority benign-mimicking families (NSL-KDD R2L, CIC Infiltration)
keep a discriminable attack score but are nearly missed at the global argmax threshold, so the much-cited
collapse is \emph{mostly} a thresholding artifact; we grade it, cleanest for the strongly scored R2L and
weaker for the only-moderately-scored Infiltration, whose score is partly representational. (iii) \emph{The
operating point is the lever, not added machinery}: on unknown R2L a budget-tuned threshold lifts recall
from \PH{comb_recall_argmax} at argmax to \PH{comb_recall_tuned} at matched analyst cost, recovering most of
what argmax misses, while an added abstention/novelty stack gives no further gain at that cost---and this
holds across three review budgets and two corpora. Selective prediction thus cuts selective risk at fixed
coverage, but only for models whose confidence ranks errors (tree ensembles, not logistic regression); and
fixed-partition confidence intervals understate uncertainty, since resampling the evaluation distribution
widens them severalfold. It is a cheap, model-agnostic safety layer whose limits we measure rather than
assume.
\end{abstract}

A network intrusion detector that emits only ``attack'' or ``benign'' gives an operator no way to tell
a confident verdict from a guess. In practice this matters twice over: alerts compete for scarce
analyst attention, and genuinely novel attacks---absent from any training corpus---are exactly the
cases a static classifier is least equipped to label. A detector that could say ``I am not sure'' on
these inputs, and defer them to a human or a secondary control (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}), would be more
useful than one forced to commit.

\textbf{The narrow, practical question.} Do off-the-shelf machine-learning intrusion detectors know
when they are wrong---producing confidence scores trustworthy enough to drive an abstention policy, and
behaving safely on attacks they have never seen? \emph{We propose no new detector; we evaluate existing
ones.} We answer empirically on NSL-KDD \cite{tavallaee2009nsl}, whose published test split deliberately
contains attack types absent from training, and on two modern flow corpora (CIC-IDS-2017 and
CIC-IDS-2018) under cross-day and cross-corpus drift (Section~\ref{sec:cic}). \textbf{The contribution is a
dissociation between two failure modes}, which we make precise with threshold-free analysis:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Over-confidence is task-dependent.} The three standard detectors are badly miscalibrated
under NSL-KDD's shift, and \emph{no} post-hoc calibrator---Platt \cite{platt1999probabilistic},
isotonic, or temperature scaling \cite{guo2017calibration}, all fit on source data---transfers; yet on
both modern corpora the same task is well calibrated (Section~\ref{sec:cal}).
\item \textbf{The benign-mimicry blind spot is an operating-point failure, not a representational one,
and it recurs across all three corpora.} Minority benign-mimicking families (R2L, Infiltration) are
nearly missed at the global decision threshold, but their attack score stays discriminative (AUROC well
above chance) and a per-family operating point recovers much of the recall; class balancing does not
help. Threshold-free, the much-cited ``detection collapse'' on unknown families is mostly an artifact of
the threshold (Sections~\ref{sec:blindspot},~\ref{sec:openset}).
\item \textbf{Selective prediction is a useful but bounded safety layer.} It cuts selective risk
substantially, but only for models whose confidence ranks errors \cite{elyaniv2010selective,geifman2017selective};
split-conformal coverage erodes under the same shift; and on the benign-mimicking blind spot the
operating point is the lever---a budget-tuned threshold recovers most of what the argmax default misses,
while an added abstention/novelty stack gives no further gain at matched analyst cost
(Sections~\ref{sec:sp},~\ref{sec:novelty}).
\end{itemize}
Our protocol and code are available at \coderepo (anonymized, reusable, open-licensed). Throughout we
follow the evaluation guidance of Arp \emph{et al.}\ \cite{arp2022dodos}: calibrators are fit only on
held-out training data, and no test information leaks into model selection. Unless stated otherwise,
NSL-KDD metrics are means over multiple seeds with 95\% CIs; because the canonical test partition is
\emph{fixed}, those CIs reflect only train/model variance, and we report separately a bootstrap over the
evaluation distribution that is roughly fivefold wider (Section~\ref{sec:cal}).

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Calibration.} Modern classifiers often output probabilities that do not match empirical
accuracy \cite{guo2017calibration,niculescu2005predicting}. Expected calibration error (ECE) summarises
this gap, and temperature/Platt scaling are common post-hoc fixes \cite{platt1999probabilistic}. These
analyses typically assume the evaluation data is drawn from the training distribution---an assumption
that fails for intrusion detection.

\textbf{Selective prediction.} A classifier with a \emph{reject option}~\cite{elyaniv2010selective}---it
may decline to answer---trades coverage (the fraction of inputs it answers) for higher accuracy on what
it does accept; the risk--coverage curve and its area \cite{geifman2017selective} quantify the
trade-off. The model's highest class probability (its confidence) is a strong baseline
score \cite{hendrycks2017baseline}.

\textbf{ML for intrusion detection.} The limits of learned detectors---especially on novel attacks and
under distribution shift---were argued early \cite{sommer2010outside} and remain a methodological
hazard \cite{arp2022dodos}. We do not propose a new detector; we ask whether existing ones know when
they are wrong.

\textbf{Unknown attacks and shift.} The failure of closed-world classifiers on inputs unlike their
training data motivates out-of-distribution and open-set detection, for which the maximum softmax
probability is the standard baseline \cite{hendrycks2017baseline}. NSL-KDD operationalises this within
a security benchmark: its test split deliberately contains attack types absent from
training~\cite{tavallaee2009nsl}, so measuring behaviour on them needs no synthetic construction. Our
family-holdout protocol complements that built-in novelty with a controlled version that makes the
``unknown'' condition causal. We deliberately reuse only off-the-shelf scores and standard metrics so
that the conclusions concern the \emph{evaluation} of detectors, not a new detector design.

\section{Methodology}
\textbf{Data.} NSL-KDD \cite{tavallaee2009nsl} provides \PH{n_train} training and \PH{n_test} test
records with 41 features (three categorical) and a binary normal/attack label; each attack maps to one
of four families (DoS, Probe, R2L, U2R). Its test split contains \PH{n_novel_types} attack \emph{types}
(\PH{n_novel_rows} records) that never appear in training---a built-in open-set test (the model must
face attack kinds it never saw). Numeric features are standardised and categorical features one-hot
encoded (turned into 0/1 indicator columns); the encoder is fit on training data only. We retain NSL-KDD
despite its age by deliberate choice: it remains the most-reported IDS benchmark---giving direct
comparability with the prior selective-prediction and ``detection-collapse'' results we revisit---and its
train--test shift is a built-in open-set stress test; we pair it with two modern flow corpora so that
dated-benchmark artefacts can be told apart from task-intrinsic effects (the task-dependence finding).

\textbf{Models.} We use scikit-learn \cite{pedregosa2011scikit} logistic regression (a linear model), a
random forest, and histogram gradient boosting (two ensembles of decision trees)---deliberately standard
detectors, since our subject is their confidence, not their architecture. The calibration analysis
(Section~\ref{sec:cal}) is on these three; the model summary (Table~\ref{tab:main}) adds a fourth family,
a neural MLP, to test whether informative confidence (low AURC) generalizes beyond trees and the linear
model.

\textbf{Calibration.} For each model we report ECE (15 equal-width bins of the predicted class
probability) raw and after three post-hoc calibrators---Platt (sigmoid) scaling, isotonic regression,
and temperature scaling \cite{guo2017calibration}---each fitted on a 20\% held-out split of the training
set, both in-distribution and on the shifted test set.

\textbf{Per-family detection and operating points.} To separate a thresholding artifact from genuine
indistinguishability, we report, besides operating-point recall, the \emph{threshold-free} detection
AUROC of the attack score (benign vs.\ family) per family. The binary target is near-balanced (46.5\%
attack), so the within-attack rarity of R2L/U2R is the only imbalance; we therefore also report recall
under \texttt{class\_weight="balanced"} and at a per-family threshold set to a 10\% benign false-positive
rate, isolating the effect of the global argmax cut. Reported separately, a bootstrap that resamples the
\emph{test partition} (B${=}1000$) gives an evaluation-distribution CI to complement the seed CIs.

\textbf{Selective prediction.} The confidence of a prediction is its maximum class probability. Sorting
the test set by confidence and accepting the most-confident fraction $c$ yields the selective risk
$r(c)$; we report the area under $r(c)$ (AURC) and $r(0.8)$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:rc}).

\textbf{Open-set protocol (LOFO).} To probe genuinely unknown attacks under control, our
leave-one-family-out protocol removes one attack family entirely from training (keeping benign traffic
and the other families), retrains, and measures detection and confidence on that family's test records.
We also report, at a global threshold giving 80\% coverage, the fraction of unknown-family records that
abstention rejects. Reported metrics are means over multiple seeds with 95\% confidence intervals.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Over-confidence under shift, and no calibrator fixes it}\label{sec:cal}
All three detectors are markedly over-confident on the benchmark's shifted test set, with ECE from
$\approx$0.16 (random forest) to $\approx$0.22 (logistic regression) (Table~\ref{tab:main}). This is
induced by distribution shift, not intrinsic: on a held-out \emph{in-distribution} split the same models
are nearly perfectly calibrated (ECE $\lesssim 0.01$). The reviewer-relevant point is that no post-hoc
calibrator fit on source data removes the shift-induced miscalibration: Platt, isotonic, and temperature
scaling all leave test ECE essentially where it was (gradient boosting \PH{histgb_ece_raw} raw vs.\
\PH{histgb_ece_cal}/\PH{histgb_ece_iso}/\PH{histgb_ece_temp}; the random forest is slightly
\emph{worse} after every one), even though each drives in-distribution ECE below 0.01
(Fig.~\ref{fig:calmethods}). The Brier score (Table~\ref{tab:main}), a proper scoring rule, tells the same
story---it too barely improves under calibration and \emph{worsens} for the random forest---so the
over-confidence is a property of the scores under shift, not a binning artefact of ECE. Calibrating to the
source distribution does not certify probabilities once the deployed distribution moves; this is why we
turn to abstention, which needs only a usable confidence \emph{ranking}, not calibrated probabilities.

\emph{How precise are these numbers?} Because the canonical KDDTest+ partition is held fixed, our seed
CIs (Table~\ref{tab:main}) capture only train/model variance and look reassuringly tight. They understate
generalization uncertainty by construction. Resampling the \emph{evaluation distribution} (bootstrap over
the test partition) widens the accuracy interval from a seed half-width near $0.001$ to
$\pm\PH{acc_eval_boot_hw}$---about fivefold---which is the honest scale of uncertainty for a single fixed
benchmark.

\subsection{The benign-mimicry blind spot is an operating-point failure}\label{sec:blindspot}
Our central finding is a dissociation, and it begins by dismantling the most attackable reading of the
blind spot. The natural worry is that R2L's near-zero detection is just an unbalanced classifier cut at
argmax (the default 0.5 decision boundary). It is not class imbalance---but it \emph{is} largely the
threshold. The attack score separates R2L from benign well above chance (AUROC \PH{pf_auroc_seen_R2L}
when R2L is seen in training, \PH{pf_auroc_unk_R2L} when it is held out; Fig.~\ref{fig:perfam}), yet at
the global argmax cut only \PH{r2l_recall_argmax} of R2L is flagged.\footnote{R2L's recall at the global
argmax cut is one quantity measured in three independent experiments---\PH{r2l_recall_argmax} here,
\PH{pf_recall_seen_R2L} in the per-family panel (Table~\ref{tab:perfam}), and \PH{det_seen_R2L} under LOFO
(Table~\ref{tab:openset}); the ${\sim}0.01$ spread is random-seed and train-split variation, not a
methodological discrepancy.} Training with
\texttt{class\_weight="balanced"} barely moves this (\PH{r2l_recall_balanced})---expected, since the
binary attack/normal task is already near-balanced---whereas a per-family threshold at a 10\% benign
false-positive rate recovers \PH{r2l_recall_fpr10}. So R2L is the hardest family, but it is
\emph{discriminable}: the catastrophic recall is one global operating point being wrong for a minority,
benign-mimicking family, not a representational dead end. This corrects an over-strong ``structural blind
spot'' reading; the blind spot is real but it lives in the decision threshold.

In plain terms: the detector can in fact tell this family from benign traffic---sort every record by its
attack score and the family piles up near the top. The trouble is the single cut-off applied to everyone.
It sits just above where this minority family lands, so almost all of them fall on the benign side even
though the ordering was right. Move the cut-off for that family, and most come back.

The same lens deflates ``detection collapse.'' Threshold-free, holding a family out of training costs
little AUROC---DoS \PH{pf_auroc_seen_DoS}$\to$\PH{pf_auroc_unk_DoS} (a drop of \PH{pf_auroc_delta_DoS}),
Probe \PH{pf_auroc_delta_Probe}, R2L \PH{pf_auroc_delta_R2L} (Table~\ref{tab:perfam})---even though
operating-point recall falls steeply. The much-cited recall collapse on unknown families is therefore
mostly the global threshold mismatching the now-unknown family, not the score losing discriminative
power. Operationally this matters: the fix is a better operating point or abstention, not richer features.

\subsection{Abstention helps---if confidence is informative}\label{sec:sp}
Abstaining on the least-confident inputs lowers selective risk---but only when the model's confidence
ranks its errors (Fig.~\ref{fig:rc}). For gradient boosting, rejecting the least-confident
20\% of inputs lowers selective risk to \PH{ms_histgb_risk80} (AURC \PH{ms_histgb_aurc}); the random
forest is similar (AURC \PH{ms_rf_aurc}). Logistic regression is the cautionary case: its confidence
barely ranks its errors (AURC \PH{ms_logreg_aurc}), so abstention buys little (\PH{ms_logreg_risk80}).
The lesson is that selective prediction is only as good as the underlying confidence signal, which here
depends strongly on the model family.

\subsection{Leave-one-family-out (LOFO): the operating-point view}\label{sec:openset}
Section~\ref{sec:blindspot} measured detection threshold-free; here is what an operator actually sees at a
fixed operating point, under the same controlled \emph{leave-one-family-out} (LOFO) protocol: for each
family $F$, remove all of $F$ from training (keeping benign and the other families), retrain, and measure
operating-point detection and abstention on $F$'s test records. At argmax, detection of DoS falls from
\PH{det_seen_DoS} (seen) to \PH{det_unseen_DoS} (unknown) and Probe from \PH{det_seen_Probe} to
\PH{det_unseen_Probe} (Table~\ref{tab:openset}); abstention at 80\% coverage then rejects
\PH{reject_unseen_DoS} and \PH{reject_unseen_Probe} of the unknown family, recovering part of the gap.
R2L is the worst case---\PH{det_unseen_R2L} detected when unknown, only \PH{reject_unseen_R2L} rejected by
abstention---because R2L overlaps the benign feature manifold for two independent benign-only detectors
alike (Fig.~\ref{fig:manifold}), and confidence abstention fires on \emph{unusual} inputs, which R2L is
not. As Section~\ref{sec:blindspot} established, this is an operating-point and manifold-overlap effect,
not an uninformative score (R2L still scores AUROC \PH{pf_auroc_unk_R2L} when unknown); the recalls here
are the deployment-time consequence of that mismatch.\footnote{U2R (train $n{=}52$, test $n{=}67$) is too
rare for its per-seed estimates to support qualitative claims; we list it in Table~\ref{tab:openset} for
completeness only and draw no conclusions from it.} The two modern-corpus studies below repeat the
protocol with the Web (CIC-2017) and Infiltration (CIC-2018) families held out.

\subsection{Two modern corpora: calibration recovers, the blind spot recurs}\label{sec:cic}
The dissociation holds across two further corpora, taking the benign-mimicry result beyond $n{=}2$.
\textbf{CIC-IDS-2017.} Within a random split this flow-based corpus (78 features; DDoS, PortScan, Web) is
far easier than NSL-KDD---gradient boosting reaches accuracy \PH{cic_histgb_acc} at ECE
\PH{cic_histgb_ece}---so over-confidence simply disappears: calibration quality is a property of the
task, not an intrinsic flaw (Table~\ref{tab:cic}). Abstention's value persists under drift: with a
cross-day split (train Friday DDoS/PortScan, test Thursday with Web unknown), which avoids the optimistic
bias of a random split~\cite{pendlebury2019tesseract}, Web detection falls from \PH{cic_web_seen} to
\PH{cic_web_unseen} and abstention rejects \PH{cic_drift_reject_web} of unknown Web---more than NSL-KDD's
R2L, because Web flows, unlike R2L, do not mimic benign traffic.

\textbf{CIC-IDS-2018 (third corpus).} Its Infiltration day (\PH{c18_n} flows) is the textbook
benign-mimicking family and shows the \emph{same qualitative} pattern as R2L: well calibrated (ECE
\PH{c18_ece}, like CIC-2017), yet the hardest family, under-detected at the global argmax cut
(\PH{c18_infil_recall_argmax}) and only partly recovered by a per-family 10\% benign-FPR threshold
(\PH{c18_infil_recall_fpr10}). We grade the dissociation rather than claim it uniform, though:
Infiltration's threshold-free score is only \emph{moderately} discriminative (AUROC \PH{c18_infil_auroc}),
well below R2L's \PH{r2l_auroc_seen}, and its recovered recall trails R2L's \PH{r2l_recall_fpr10}. So the
operating-point reading is cleanest for R2L; for Infiltration part of the miss is \emph{representational}
---the score itself is weaker---not purely a thresholding artifact. The honest three-corpus picture is
therefore a \emph{graded} dissociation: calibration failure is task-specific, and a single global
operating point under-detects minority benign-mimicking families whose score stays at least partly
discriminable---most cleanly when that score is strong (R2L), less so when it is weak (Infiltration).

\textbf{Cross-corpus transfer.} Training on CIC-2017 and testing on CIC-2018 (the \PH{xfer_n_common}
CICFlowMeter features whose names match across releases) is the strongest external-validity test: the
phenomena transfer but the guarantees do not. Accuracy falls to \PH{xfer_acc}, calibration collapses (ECE
\PH{xfer_ece}), and abstention recovers far less than within-corpus (selective risk
\PH{xfer_risk_full}$\to$\PH{xfer_risk80}), with Infiltration again the blind spot (detected
\PH{xfer_infil_detect}; $K{=}5$ seeds, CI half-width $\le\PH{xfer_maxhw}$).

\subsection{Which signal, and can coverage be guaranteed?}
Is max-softmax (MSP) the right abstention signal, and can abstention carry a formal guarantee? On NSL-KDD,
MSP is a strong, well-rounded default among classical signals: random-forest ensemble disagreement is
statistically indistinguishable from it (paired $\Delta$AURC \PH{ms_dmsp_rf_disagreement}, CI includes
0), Mahalanobis distance ranks errors marginally better (\PH{ms_dmsp_mahalanobis}) but separates unknown
attacks worse, and $k$NN is worse on both (\PH{ms_dmsp_knn}); the leverage is in the abstention framework,
not the estimator. We compare classical signals only---energy- and ODIN-style scores assume a neural net,
which we deliberately do not use, so our claim is scoped to off-the-shelf tabular models. For a guarantee,
split conformal prediction~\cite{angelopoulos2023conformal} attains target $0.90$ coverage
in-distribution (\PH{ms_conf_indist}) but only \PH{ms_conf_shift} under the train--test
shift~\cite{tibshirani2019}---the conformal analogue of the Section~\ref{sec:cal} calibration failure;
restoring it needs shift-aware weighting with estimable likelihood ratios, impractical for genuine
zero-days.

\subsection{A benign-only novelty stage partially closes the blind spot}\label{sec:novelty}
Confidence-based abstention barely flags R2L (Section~\ref{sec:openset}) because such attacks lie on the
benign feature manifold (Fig.~\ref{fig:manifold}). The apt detector for that regime is a \emph{benign-only} density model---a
one-class novelty detector, distinct from the two-class Mahalanobis used above for error ranking. We fit
an Isolation Forest~\cite{liu2008isolation} and a one-class SVM~\cite{scholkopf2001oneclass} on benign
records only. At a 10\% benign false-positive budget the Isolation Forest recovers
\PH{nov_if_rec_U2R_fp10} of U2R and \PH{nov_if_rec_R2L_fp10} of R2L (the one-class SVM
\PH{nov_ocsvm_rec_U2R_fp10} and \PH{nov_ocsvm_rec_R2L_fp10}; means over $K{=}8$ seeds, 95\% CI
half-width $\le\PH{nov_maxhw}$)---against abstention's $\approx\PH{reject_unseen_R2L}$ for unknown R2L,
a clear if partial gain. The AUROC ceilings
(Fig.~\ref{fig:novelty}: R2L \PH{nov_if_auroc_R2L}, U2R \PH{nov_if_auroc_U2R}) show the limit is real but
not absolute: R2L is hardest because its support most overlaps benign, so no benign-only detector fully
recovers it, yet a novelty stage is a cheap, measured complement---abstention and novelty catch
different failure modes (confident errors vs.\ benign-mimicking attacks).

\emph{The recipe, re-baselined honestly.} Stacking looks like a win only against the argmax cut, a bad
threshold for a minority family. We instead compare three policies on held-out (unknown) R2L at the
\emph{same} review budget (\PH{comb_review}): argmax, a global threshold \emph{tuned} to that budget, and
the full stack (argmax/abstention/novelty). Argmax is blind (\PH{comb_recall_argmax}); a tuned threshold
recovers \PH{comb_recall_tuned}; the full stack reaches only \PH{comb_recall_union}, \emph{below} the
tuned threshold at matched cost. Two distinct resamplings agree on no gain: the seed-paired gap is
\PH{comb_delta_union_tuned} (over the six training seeds), and resampling the evaluation distribution of a
fixed model gives a gap of \PH{comb_delta_boot_mid} (95\% CI
$[\PH{comb_delta_boot_lo},\PH{comb_delta_boot_hi}]$, upper bound ${\approx}0$; Fig.~\ref{fig:combined}).
The recovery is the operating point, not the
gates; even so, the tuned threshold still misses about half of unknown R2L (the stack about 60\%). This is
not a single-corpus result: Section~\ref{sec:validatefix} repeats the comparison across three review
budgets on both unknown R2L and cross-corpus Infiltration, with the same conclusion.

\subsection{Validating the fix across budgets and corpora}\label{sec:validatefix}
The re-baselined gate (Section~\ref{sec:novelty}) compared the stack to a budget-matched tuned threshold
at a single operating point; we now sweep the budget and add a second corpus to check the conclusion is
not a single-point artefact. At a matched analyst review budget $b\in\{5,10,20\}\%$ (benign FPR), we
compare a single global threshold \emph{tuned} to $b$ against the
classifier\,$\cup$\,abstention\,$\cup$\,novelty stack, on unknown R2L (NSL-KDD, family held out) and
cross-corpus Infiltration (CIC-2018, the second benign-mimicking family), over $K{=}6$ seeds
(Fig.~\ref{fig:validatefix}). The stack beats the tuned threshold in \emph{one} of six budget$\times$corpus
cells (CIC at $b{=}5\%$, $\Delta{=}\PH{vf_cic2018_infil_b5_delta}$); it loses the other five, decisively on
unknown R2L ($\Delta{=}\PH{vf_nslkdd_r2l_b10_delta}$ at $b{=}10\%$, $\PH{vf_nslkdd_r2l_b20_delta}$ at
$20\%$). At equal analyst cost the novelty stage adds no recall a budget-tuned threshold does not already
deliver. The reason is structural: on a benign-mimicking family the novelty and classifier scores are
positively dependent---both fire on the same atypical-but-benign-looking flows---so the union spends extra
benign review for little marginal recall, whereas moving the single threshold spends the whole budget on
the most informative score. Across budgets and corpora, the lever is the operating point, not the stack.

\emph{Is the comparison fair to the stack?} We tune the stack \emph{favourably}, not as a strawman. Its
gates take conventional, generous settings---abstention at the standard 80\% coverage and the benign-only
novelty gate at the benign-FPR operating point that maximises its own held-out R2L recall---and we then
allocate the tuned-threshold baseline the \emph{same} total benign review budget the stack realises, so
neither side is advantaged on analyst cost. The negative result is thus conservative: even a
favourably-tuned stack fails to beat a single threshold at equal budget, and a harder-tuned threshold
(which we do not use) would only widen the gap.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
Selective prediction is a cheap, model-agnostic safety layer: at the cost of deferring a minority of
inputs it materially lowers the error among those acted upon, and it partially flags unknown attacks.
But our results bound the claim. First, the value of abstention is inherited from the confidence
signal, which is strong for the tree ensembles, still useful for the neural MLP, and poor for the linear
model (a spectrum of confidence quality, not a linear/nonlinear dichotomy). Second, post-hoc calibration
on source data does not survive distribution shift, so calibrated probabilities should not be trusted
as deployment-time guarantees. We should be plain about what is and is not novel here: the contribution
is the \emph{diagnosis}---that the much-cited ``detection collapse'' on unknown families is largely a
thresholding artifact over a score that stays discriminable---not a new defense mechanism. The remedy it
implies is deliberately simple (a per-family operating point plus a benign-only novelty stage), and we
present it as such; the natural reaction ``so just use a better threshold'' is agreement with our point,
not a rebuttal to it. Third, and most important operationally, a single global \emph{argmax} operating
point silently under-detects minority benign-mimicking families (R2L, Infiltration) even though their
score is discriminable; the fix is to set the operating point to the analyst budget---a per-family or
budget-tuned threshold, not richer features---and an added abstention/novelty stack does not beat that
tuned threshold at matched cost (Section~\ref{sec:novelty}).

\subsection{Recommendations}
The diagnosis turns into concrete guidance, in order. \emph{(1)} Pick a model whose confidence
\emph{ranks} errors---low validation AURC, strongest for the tree ensembles, still useful for the neural
MLP, and poor for logistic regression (Table~\ref{tab:main}); calibrated probabilities are neither
required nor, under shift, achievable post-hoc. \emph{(2)} Set the operating point to the analyst review budget \emph{before}
reaching for novelty detection: a budget-tuned (ideally per-family) threshold recovers
\PH{comb_recall_tuned} of unknown R2L versus \PH{comb_recall_argmax} at argmax, while an abstention/novelty
stack adds no recall at matched cost (Section~\ref{sec:validatefix}). \emph{(3)} Set selective coverage
from the risk--coverage curve to a target precision, route the abstained minority to a secondary control,
and monitor the abstention rate as a cheap, label-free drift
signal~\cite{jordaney2017transcend,yang2021cade}. \emph{(4)} Report \emph{bootstrap} confidence intervals
over the evaluation distribution, not just fixed-partition seed intervals, which understate uncertainty
severalfold on a single fixed benchmark.

\subsection{Limitations}
NSL-KDD is dated and hand-crafted, so absolute numbers will differ on modern corpora;
the mechanisms we isolate---over-confidence under shift, model-dependent confidence quality, and the
operating-point blind spot on benign-mimicking families---reproduce on CIC-IDS-2017 and 2018, so they are
not specific to it. We study binary detection with a
single abstention signal (maximum class probability); richer signals and the multi-class
attack-typing setting are natural extensions. We evaluate a cross-day split and a cross-corpus transfer
(CIC-2017$\to$2018) on shared features; a full time-ordered evaluation, the label-corrected
CIC-2017~\cite{engelen2021troubleshooting}, and standardized NetFlow-v2 multi-network transfer (a common
schema avoiding our 27-feature restriction) remain future work.

\section{Conclusion}
Off-the-shelf intrusion detectors are over-confident under NSL-KDD's shift and not fixable by post-hoc
calibration (Platt, isotonic, or temperature), yet well-calibrated on two modern corpora---calibration
need is task-dependent. The second failure recurs on all three corpora but is an \emph{operating-point}
one: a single global threshold silently under-detects minority benign-mimicking families whose attack
score is in fact discriminable, so the much-cited ``detection collapse'' on unknown families is mostly a
thresholding artifact. A reject option lowers selective risk where confidence ranks errors, and simply
tuning the operating point to the analyst budget recovers much of the benign-mimicking traffic the argmax
default hides---while a stacked novelty gate adds no further recall at matched cost. Selective prediction
is worth adding to ML intrusion detection, provided its confidence signal and its blind spots are
measured, not assumed.
